Discharge Tube Lab

Build molecules from atoms, load them into a gas-discharge tube, and read the light they give off. Atoms emit sharp lines. Molecules emit bands. And the discharge itself breaks molecules into fragments that glow too.

    What you're seeing

      Why molecules don't just show their atoms' lines

      Atoms have only electronic energy levels, so each transition gives one sharp line at λ = hc/ΔE.

      Molecules also vibrate and rotate. Each electronic transition splits into many vibrational bands (v′ → v″), and each band into dozens of rotational lines. The lines crowd together at a band head and spread out on one side, so molecular spectra look like shaded combs instead of single lines.

      The discharge is violent. Fast electrons excite molecules, ionize them (N₂⁺, CO⁺) and break bonds. Fragments like CH, OH, CN and C₂ glow with their own bands, freed atoms give atomic lines, and atoms can recombine into new species: an N₂ + O₂ mixture produces NO, just as lightning does.

      Turn up discharge power and watch parent-molecule bands fade while atomic lines and ion bands grow. Strong bonds (N≡N, C≡O) survive longest.

      Investigation prompts
      1. Build a single H atom, then H₂. Which features are new in H₂? Why does H₂ show a smooth continuum below 400 nm when atomic H never does? (Tap it and look at the lower-state curve.)
      2. Calculate the photon energy of Hβ (486.1 nm) in eV and kJ/mol. Check it against the Rydberg equation for n = 4 → 2.
      3. Build N₂ and slide discharge power from low to high. Rank the N₂, N₂⁺ and N-atom features by how they respond, and explain the trend using the N≡N bond energy (945 kJ/mol).
      4. Compare CH₄, C₂H₆, C₂H₄ and C₂H₂. How does the C₂ Swan band at 516.5 nm change relative to the CH band at 431.4 nm? What does that suggest about which bonds are present?
      5. CO₂ has no “CO₂ band” in the visible. Identify the species responsible for the strongest visible features and explain where they came from.
      6. Load “Titan haze (N₂ + CH₄)”. Neither gas contains a C–N bond. Which feature shows that one formed in the tube?
      7. Use Mystery tube: identify the gas from its spectrum alone, then build it until the match score reaches 100%.
      About the model and sources

      Atomic line wavelengths (in air) are from the NIST Atomic Spectra Database. Molecular band-head wavelengths follow standard tables (Pearse & Gaydon, The Identification of Molecular Spectra), rounded to 0.1 nm; a few weaker heads (CO⁺, CO₂⁺ A–X, O₂⁺, NH₂) are approximate. Relative intensities, rotational structure, and the fragmentation and recombination rates are simplified models chosen to behave like a real low-pressure discharge. Real tubes vary with pressure, current and impurities. The energy-level diagrams are schematic, not to scale.

      Lewis structures use octet filling by electronegativity; formal charges are shown where they are not zero.
